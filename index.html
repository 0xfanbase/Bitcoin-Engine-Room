block height · loading…

Block height counts every block Bitcoin has ever mined, in order — the number shown is block #N since the very first block in 2009. A new block arrives roughly every 10 minutes on average, and it's the clock the halving countdown runs on.

Source: mempool.space (live) · blockchain.info (fallback)

time since last block

This rail sweeps once for every new block that actually arrives, and otherwise creeps only very slightly between them -- its position (and the ticking "Xm Ys" reading next to it) is roughly how much of the ~10-minute average block interval has elapsed since the last one. A second read on the same clock the block-height odometer runs on, not a separate metric.

Source: computed from mempool.space's live block-arrival timestamp

The current market price of one bitcoin in US dollars. This site cross-checks its primary price feed against a second, independent source every time it updates — if they ever disagree by too much, it's flagged in Engine Health below, not silently hidden.

Source: mempool.space (live) · CoinGecko / Coinbase (cross-check + fallback)

Blocks to halving

The number of blocks left to mine before the mining reward is cut in half again — this happens automatically every 210,000 blocks, roughly every four years. It's a countdown built into the protocol itself, not an estimate.

Source: computed live from the current block height

—
Est. halving date

A calendar-date estimate for when block 1,050,000 will be mined, based on the network's current average block time. Because block times vary block to block, this date is a moving estimate, not a fixed appointment.

Source: computed from live block height + Bitcoin's fixed block-reward schedule

Gauges

What do the status colors mean?

LIVE — streaming in real time over a live connection to the fastest available source.

DELAYED — still real, current data, just pulled over a slower polled or fallback connection instead of the fastest live one.

DAILY — refreshed once a day by this site's own pipeline rather than continuously — a healthy, on-schedule reading, not a problem.

STALE — every source in the chain failed; you're looking at the last successfully saved snapshot, with its age shown, not a live or current number.

Hash Rate

—
—
—

An estimate of the total computing power miners are pointing at securing the Bitcoin network right now. The higher it is, the more expensive it would be for anyone to attack or rewrite the blockchain — it's Bitcoin's real-world security budget, made visible.

Source: mempool.space (live) · blockchain.info (history)

Difficulty · Retarget

—
—
—

Difficulty is a self-adjusting number that keeps new blocks arriving roughly every 10 minutes, no matter how much mining power joins or leaves — it retargets automatically every 2,016 blocks, about two weeks. Rising difficulty means more miners are competing for the reward.

Source: mempool.space (live) · blockchain.info (fallback)

Fees (sat/vB)

—
fastest · 30min · economy
—

What you pay, in satoshis per virtual byte, to get a transaction into the next block. The three tiers shown are the fee levels needed to hit fastest / 30-minute / economy confirmation times — fees rise and fall with how crowded the mempool is.

Source: mempool.space (live only, no fallback)

Mempool

—
pending transactions
—

The waiting room: every valid transaction that's been broadcast but not yet confirmed in a block sits here until a miner picks it up. A big mempool usually means the network is busy and fees are rising to compete for the next block's limited space.

Source: mempool.space, live WebSocket feed

Circulating Supply

—
—
—

How many bitcoin have been mined so far, out of the hard-capped 21,000,000 that will ever exist. New issuance is cut in half at every halving, so the pace of new coins entering circulation keeps shrinking on a fixed, published schedule.

Source: blockchain.info (live + history) · block-subsidy schedule (last-resort fallback)

Price Models

Power Law Corridor

—

—

A long-running model, published by Giovanni Santostasi, that fits Bitcoin's price history to a power-law curve: a straight line when both price and time are on log scales. Cruise is that fitted line.

Idle and Redline bound the range price has actually spent ~95% of its history in, measured as the spread of past prices around the line (the inner band holds ~68%). They are descriptive, not confidence intervals, and not a prediction of where price goes next. The dashed fan to the right is how uncertain the line itself is; the dotted line is the same model fit only to data since 2017, which is flatter.

Source: model published by Giovanni Santostasi; refit nightly against this site's own price history. Uncertainty, backtest and method: MODEL_METHODOLOGY.md

4-Year Cycle Overlay

—

Stacks each Bitcoin halving cycle on top of the others, plotting % price performance against days since that cycle's own halving, so different cycles land on the same chart, comparably.

Bitcoin's four-year halving cycle has historically rhymed with big price cycles — this is a way to see whether the current cycle is tracking, lagging, or outrunning past ones, not a guarantee it keeps rhyming.

Source: computed from this site's own historical price data, sliced at each halving-epoch boundary

Growth multiple since each halving-day price, log scale, so every cycle reads on one chart. Three earlier cycles are a tiny sample -- a rhyme, not a rule. #cycle

Mayer Multiple · 200-Week MA

—

Mayer Multiple is today's price divided by its own 200-day moving average — 1.0 means price sits exactly at that average; well above means it's run up fast, well below means it's pulled back hard. The 200-week moving average is a much slower version of the same idea, using 200 weeks instead of 200 days.

Mayer Multiple is often read as flagging overheated (high) or oversold (low) conditions, but this site's own point-in-time test found it has not reliably done so a year ahead (see Track Record). The 200-week average has historically acted as a long-term floor — price has rarely stayed below it for long.

Source: computed nightly from this site's own historical price data

Top: price and its 200-week simple moving average. Bottom: Mayer Multiple = price / 200-day average; shaded zones are the traditional <0.8 and >2.4 readings. #mayer

Track Record

—

How well would the Power Law Corridor have done if you had used it in the past? On the first of every month since 2013, the exact model on this page is refit using only the prices known before that day, and its forecast is compared with what actually happened 1, 2 and 4 years later.

"Miss" is the typical factor between forecast and reality: ×2.0 means off by about a factor of two, in either direction. "Price stays flat" is the naive benchmark. Monthly forecasts overlap heavily, so the honest sample size is the number of independent windows, shown in the last column — and it is small.

What do these checks mean?

Every day this site runs seven automated checks against its own data and forecasts before publishing, rolling up to one overall result shown in the chip above: PASS (nothing found), WARN (worth a look, not urgent), or FAIL (a real problem). A findings list only appears above when a check flags something — a clean day shows none. Here's what each one actually looks for:

continuity — is there a gap in the daily history, a date with no saved snapshot?

cross_source_variance — do two independent price sources agree with each other today, and does the committed price history agree with an independent exchange (Bitstamp) day by day?

model_drift — did last night's price-model refit change suspiciously overnight? (Usually bad input data, not a broken market.)

staleness — how long has any gauge been showing old, non-live data instead of a fresh reading?

sanity_replay — re-checking the last month of saved data for impossible values, in case something slipped through the day it was saved.

site_integrity — is the site itself intact — no broken files, no missing pages, no runaway file sizes?

forecast_calibration — are the model's published bands actually holding up? Checks the walk-forward backtest and every matured entry in the live forecast ledger.
